## Introduction

In the technology and finance sectors, news moves relentlessly. When a major event occurs—a sudden executive departure or an unannounced pivot—the initial wave of information rarely comes from a verified announcement. Instead, it arrives through a torrent of commentary. A rumor surfaces, a prominent investor tweets, and aggregators flood with overlapping narratives.

For operators and researchers, this creates a severe challenge. The volume of commentary creates an illusion of certainty. Yet, this consensus is often built on a fragile foundation—or none at all. To navigate fast-moving company news, one must draw a hard line between primary sources and commentary. Commentary tells you what people are reacting to; primary sources tell you what actually happened. Relying on the former leads to decisions based on noise, while insisting on the latter builds a durable foundation. This article explores why an evidence-first approach is the only sustainable way to process market changes.

## The Illusion of Commentary

The modern information ecosystem is optimized for engagement. Platforms like Reddit, Hacker News, and specialized aggregators are designed to surface what captures attention. They provide immediate, visceral reactions and speculative analysis.

However, commentary is inherently derivative. It takes an input—often a rumor or a leaked memo—and amplifies it. The problem is that commentary is frequently mistaken for evidence.

Consider a scenario where a startup is rumored to be facing a down round. A single speculative post might be picked up by a newsletter, cited by a podcast, and generate a flurry of social posts. The volume of discussion is massive, but the number of primary sources is zero.

This creates a dangerous feedback loop. The more a topic is discussed, the more credible it appears. But when you trace the narrative back, you often find a void. The commentary has laundered a rumor into a "fact." Relying on this derivative layer means reacting to imagination rather than reality.

## Defining the Primary Source

If commentary is noise, what constitutes the signal? In business, a primary source is a direct, original record of an event. It is the raw material from which factual claims are derived, untainted by secondary interpretation.

In practical terms, primary sources include:

1.  **Regulatory Filings:** The gold standard of corporate disclosure. SEC forms, such as 8-Ks and 10-Qs, carry legal weight. When a company states a material fact here, it is a primary source.
2.  **Direct Issuer Announcements:** Official press releases and investor relations (IR) pages. While biased toward the company's interests, they are definitive records of public claims.
3.  **Legal and Government Records:** Court dockets, patent grants, federal register notices, and municipal land-use filings (e.g., a city council agenda for a data center). These provide unvarnished evidence.
4.  **Original Reporting:** Journalism that cites named, on-the-record sources or verifiable documents, distinct from blog posts that merely summarize.

The defining characteristic of a primary source is its proximity to the event. It does not rely on the aggregate sentiment of a message board. It is the document or direct statement itself.

## The "Cite or Kill" Standard

Understanding the difference is only the first step. The real challenge is operationalizing this distinction. How do you process thousands of daily data points without being overwhelmed?

The answer is a ruthless standard: Cite or Kill.

In an evidence-first approach, a claim cannot be published unless explicitly supported by primary sources. A robust standard requires independent corroboration. If a claim cannot be backed by at least two independent primary sources, it must be killed.

This acts as a crucial filter. When a wave of commentary hits, the "Cite or Kill" rule forces you to ask: Where is the official announcement? If the only supporting evidence is a thread on Hacker News, the claim fails. Missing evidence never becomes a positive claim.

Implementing this means accepting you will not be the first to publish a rumor. You miss the viral surge of unverified news. However, the trade-off is durability. When a claim survives, it is built on verifiable reality.

## Attention is Not Evidence

A common pitfall is conflating attention with evidence. Because we can measure trending topics and social velocity, it is tempting to use these metrics as proxies for truth. If a topic is trending, there must be something to it.

This is a fundamental error. Attention is a measure of interest, not fact.

A spike in mentions on social platforms or upvotes on an aggregator like Digg can raise the priority of an investigation. It tells you where the market's eyes are focused. But it does not provide the proof to validate a claim. Attention metrics can tell you people believe a CEO is stepping down; they cannot prove the resignation.

Similarly, prediction markets gauge consensus probabilities. However, they are not primary evidence. A 90% probability on a betting platform that a company will be acquired reflects sentiment, not a substitute for a merger agreement.

Attention and commentary can serve as a discovery layer, pointing toward what needs verifying. But verification must rely on primary, material evidence.

## Engineering for Truth

How does one build a system that prioritizes primary sources?

First, constrain your inputs. If you ingest every social feed, your system will be overwhelmed. Instead, prioritize direct pipelines to regulatory bodies, official company feeds, and government APIs. Ensure your discovery corpus is an evidence-qualified subset of reachable routes.

Second, separate observation from inference. Record the raw fact as stated by the primary source. If an IR page announces a 10% workforce reduction, that is the event. The broader business inference must be kept distinct. Inference is interpretation; the event is fact.

Third, embrace versioned memory and public ledgers. When you make a directional call based on primary sources, log it. When new evidence emerges, do not retroactively rewrite history. Issue a correction that cites the prior one. Maintain a public hit-rate ledger that calibrates confidence over time.

By engineering a system that demands citations, measures outcomes, and logs history, you shift focus from being the fastest to being the most reliable.

## Conclusion

In fast-moving company news, initial commentary is loud, chaotic, and incomplete. It is driven by the desire to be first. For those making consequential decisions, playing this game is a losing proposition.

Differentiating between primary sources and commentary is a practical necessity. By insisting on actual material evidence—regulatory filings, direct announcements, court records—and rejecting the illusion of consensus, you protect yourself from noise.

The "Cite or Kill" standard requires patience. But the reward is durability. When you base your understanding on primary sources, your conclusions are verifiable, your track record is measurable, and your signal remains high long after the commentary fades.

## Practical Next Action

Audit your own information diet today. Take the last three major company news stories you reacted to. Trace each back to its origin. Did your information come from an SEC filing or an original reported document? Or did it come from a newsletter summarizing a tweet?

If relying heavily on the latter, adjust your inputs. Bookmark the IR pages of the companies you care about. Learn to read 8-Ks. Adopt a personal "Cite or Kill" rule: before acting on news, find the primary source document.
